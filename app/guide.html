What you can find out about a collection

This program does one thing at its base: it asks a pre-trained model to look at each of your images and describe it as a long list of numbers. Images that the model finds similar get similar numbers. Everything else here — every map, every group, every ranking — is a different way of questioning that one list.

The page below explains, in plain terms, what each function actually tells you, when it is the right one to reach for, and what it cannot do. No feature here is magic, and several of them are easy to misread; where that is true, it says so.

The map

The model describes each image with hundreds of numbers. A screen has two dimensions. Everything in this section is a different way of squashing the first into the second — and each way loses something different.

Scatter plot (UMAP / t-SNE)

Embedding Projection

"Which of my images does the model consider alike?"

Each dot is one image, placed so that images the model finds similar land near each other. Dense knots are groups of pictures the model sees as variations on one thing; empty space between knots means it sees a real difference.

This is the view to start from. Switch on thumbnails and you can read the groups directly: you will usually recognise what a cluster has in common within a few seconds of looking at it.

Read with care. The two axes mean nothing. Left-to-right is not a quantity, and neither is up-and-down — they are just the directions the algorithm happened to flatten along. Distances between nearby dots are fairly trustworthy; distances between far-apart ones are not.

Named axes

Embedding Projection · optional

"Where does each image sit between this idea and that one?"

Instead of letting the algorithm choose the axes, you write them. Type "a portrait of a person" at one end and "a landscape" at the other, do the same for the vertical axis, and the collection is laid out on two scales you named and can read.

This is the answer to the complaint above. Here a position does mean something: an image halfway up really is halfway between the two ideas you gave, as far as the model is concerned.

Read with care. If your two axes describe similar things, everything collapses onto a diagonal and the plot tells you nothing. The app measures this and warns you when it happens.

Grid mosaic

Embedding Projection · view mode

"Let me actually see every image, without any of them hiding each other."

The scatter plot's worst habit is piling images on top of one another exactly where they are most interesting. The mosaic gives every image its own equal cell in a grid, keeping similar images near each other as far as a grid allows.

Use it when you want to browse rather than measure — it is the best view for spotting something by eye across the whole collection.

Read with care. The grid has to distort distances to fit everything into equal cells. Neighbours are still meaningful; the exact gaps are not.

Colour by a concept

Embedding Projection · optional

"Where in my collection does this idea occur?"

Type a phrase — "a ruin", "night", "strong contrast" — and every image is shaded by how much of it the model sees. Cold colours are the least, warm the most.

This is a different question from search. Search gives you the top thirty; this shows the idea's distribution. You may discover it is spread evenly across two clusters, or concentrated in one corner of a single group — which search would never have told you.

Read with care. The colours are relative to your collection, not absolute. The reddest image is the most X here, which in a collection with no X at all still means very little.

Groups and order

Four analyses that run over the stored numbers and answer with a list rather than a picture.

Clusters

Embedding Projection

"Split my collection into groups."

The collection is divided into a chosen number of groups, each with a centre. For each group the app can show you its most typical image — the one closest to that centre — and how tight the group is.

A tight group is a real theme. A loose one is usually a leftover bin: the images that did not belong anywhere else got put together.

Read with care. This method always returns exactly the number of groups you asked for, whether or not the collection has any. The cohesion figure is what tells you if a group is real.

Duplicates and near-duplicates

Embedding Projection · Analysis

"Do I have the same picture twice?"

Finds images the model considers all but identical, and groups them. It catches what a file comparison misses: the same work rescanned, recropped, recoloured, or saved at a different size.

Groups are listed most-certain first, and a group assembled through a chain (A resembles B, B resembles C, but A and C were never directly compared) is flagged as such, because those are the ones worth checking by eye.

Read with care. "Near-identical to the model" can mean two genuinely different works in the same series. Always look before you delete anything.

Outliers

Embedding Projection · Analysis

"What in here doesn't belong with the rest?"

Every image is ranked by how far it is from its own nearest neighbours. The top of that ranking is the images with the least company.

In practice this surfaces two useful things at once: genuinely unusual works, and mistakes — the scan that went wrong, the file that is not really part of the collection, the blank page.

Read with care. Being an outlier says nothing about quality or interest. It says only that nothing else here is much like it.

Compare two images

Compare · also as a quick look on Embedding Projection

"How related are these two, really?"

A bare similarity number is close to meaningless — whether 0.72 is high depends entirely on the model and the collection. So the comparison adds context: where each image ranks in the other's list of neighbours, and where the pair sits among all pairs in the collection.

The revealing case is a one-sided pair: A considers B one of its closest relatives while B barely notices A. That usually means A is unusual and B is a crowd-pleaser.

How honest is the map?

Embedding Projection · Analysis · Map honesty

"How much is this layout making up?"

Every projection flattens a space of hundreds of dimensions onto a page, and something always gets bent. This measures how much. For a sample of image pairs it compares the distance the model sees with the distance the map shows, and draws the two against each other. A faithful map produces a narrow rising band; a distorted one produces a cloud.

Two numbers come with it. Rank correlation says how often the map gets the order of two distances right — whether what looks closer really is closer. Stress says how much it bends them: near 0 the distances can be read directly, above about 0.2 the layout is also telling a story of its own, and the right response is to read neighbourhoods rather than gaps.

The two lists under the plot are the pairs the map describes worst, and they fail in opposite directions. Drawn closer than they are is the dangerous one: the map invites a comparison the model never made. Pulled apart though alike is the opposite — a likeness the layout hides, and a good reason to open the neighbour graph. Clicking a pair walks the chain between them.

The chain between two images

Compare

"What lies between these two?"

Similar images answers what else looks like one picture, and Compare answers how related two of them are. Neither says what sits in between. Pick two images on the Compare page and the chain walks the nearest-neighbour graph from one to the other, showing every step: usually five or six pictures that turn the first into the second.

Each step carries the similarity of that single hop, and it is normal for every hop to be far higher than the similarity of the two ends. That is the point: two works at 0.38 can be joined by a run of 0.7 steps, and the pictures in between are what the model used to get across — a shared subject, a palette, a way of placing a figure on the canvas.

Two things to keep in mind. The route depends on k: a small k forces long detours and can leave the two images in separate parts of the graph, in which case the panel says so instead of implying there is no relationship. And the chain is the cheapest route through the graph, not the only one — a different k will often find another, equally honest.

Finding things

The models in the CLIP family were trained on images together with their captions, so they place a picture and a description of it in the same place. That is what makes the next two possible.

Search by description

Embedding Projection

"Show me the images that look like this description."

Type what you are looking for in ordinary language — "a stormy sea at night", "two people arguing" — and the collection is ranked against it. Nothing needs to be tagged or labelled beforehand.

It works best with plain descriptive phrases. Proper names, dates, artists and technical art-historical terms usually fail, because the captions it learned from rarely contained them.

Similar images

Embedding Projection · Analysis

"More like this one."

Pick any image and get its closest relatives, ranked. This is measured in the full space of numbers, not on the flattened map, so it is more trustworthy than judging by what looks near it on the plot.

Searching with several images

Embedding Projection · Similar images

"More like these — but not like that"

Search by description needs the thing named. Similar images needs one example, which makes the search as narrow as that single picture. This asks with several at once: press + on any result to put it in the query, and the collection is ranked by the direction those images have in common. Set an image to − and it is subtracted instead — what you want, with the part you did not want removed.

It exists for the normal condition in front of a visual collection: knowing what you are looking for and being unable to name it. A palette, a kind of light, a way of placing a figure on the canvas — three examples say it where no phrase does.

Two readings to keep in mind. The panel reports how far the chosen images agree: near 1 they point the same way, and low agreement means they are pulling apart, so the results will be weak whatever comes back. And after a subtraction the scores drop across the board, because the query is no longer pointing at anything in particular — read the order, not the value.

The graph

A separate view that draws a line between each image and the handful it is genuinely closest to. Because the links are computed in the full space, the graph knows things the flattened map cannot show.

Communities

Neighbor graph · Structure

"How is the collection organised, according to the links themselves?"

The algorithm looks for sets of images that link to each other far more than they link outward, and keeps refining until no single move improves the division. Click a community and the graph shows that group alone.

The value is that this is a second, independent opinion. It is reached without the 2D map and without the cluster count you chose. When it agrees with the clusters, the grouping is solid; when it disagrees, one of the two has distorted something and it is worth finding out which.

Read with care. The modularity figure says how clear-cut the division is. Below about 0.3 the collection is fairly uniform and the communities are weak; above 0.5 the groups are strongly distinct.

Bridges and load-bearing images

Neighbor graph · Structure

"What is holding two groups together?"

A bridge is a single link that, if removed, would split the graph in two. A load-bearing image is one whose removal would do the same. Both are drawn in amber.

These are often the most interesting images in a collection: the work that belongs to two worlds at once, and is the only thing connecting them.

Links that expose the map

Neighbor graph · edge colour

"Where is the flat map lying to me?"

Every link carries two numbers that ought to agree: how long it is on screen, and how similar the two images actually are. Colouring the links by the disagreement between them turns the graph into a check on the projection.

The warm links are strongly related images that the map pushed far apart — precisely the places where trusting the scatter plot would mislead you.

Hubs and isolated images

Neighbor graph

"Which images is everything else clustered around?"

Colouring nodes by how many links they have shows the collection's centres of gravity. A hub is an image many others consider one of their closest; an isolated node is one nothing reaches.

The number of neighbours per image (k) is the control that matters most here. Low values break the collection into small fragments; high values merge everything into one mass. Raise it until the structure you care about appears.

Questions in words

Three functions that answer with language or with a comparison rather than with a picture of the collection. They live in their own view because they are a different kind of question.

What separates two groups

Lenses

"I can see these two groups differ. In what way?"

Take two sets of images — two clusters, or the most and least "X" in the collection — and the app reports the difference between them as a list of words: these terms lean towards A, these towards B.

Anything the two groups have in common cancels out, which is what makes the answer useful: it names what separates them, not what they are.

Read with care. Two things. The answer can only use words from the vocabulary list, and the model will always pick the closest term offered even when none of them fits — so edit that list to suit your collection. And check the centroid similarity reported with the answer: if the two groups nearly coincide, a long list of distinguishing words means very little.

Concept arithmetic

Lenses

"Images like this one, but more X and less Y."

Start from an image, add an idea, subtract another, and the collection is ranked against the result. The idea strength slider decides the balance: at zero you simply get the starting image's neighbours, and as you raise it the words take over.

Scores are shown in standard deviations rather than as a similarity, because what is being combined is how far above the collection's average each image sits on each term — the only scale on which an image and a phrase can fairly be added together.

Read with care. This is an instrument for thinking, not a reliable operation. The space encodes web captions, not a grammar. It works often enough to be illuminating and fails often enough that you should always look at what comes back.

Where two models disagree

Lenses

"Which of my images have a gap between their subject and their style?"

Run the same folder twice, once with a CLIP-family model and once with DINOv2. The first groups images by what they show; the second by how they look. Then compare the two.

Neither is wrong — they answer different questions — and the images they place most differently are the interesting residue: a landscape painted like a portrait, a sacred scene handled as everyday genre. Neither model can point at these on its own, because each sees only its own answer. This information exists nowhere except in the comparison.

Read with care. The comparison is made on neighbourhoods, not positions. Two models' coordinates cannot be compared at all — they are unrelated — but "which images does this model think are closest to this one" means the same thing in both.

What none of it can do

The limits worth knowing before you trust any of this

Every function on this page rests on the same foundation, and inherits the same limits.

  • The model learned from pictures with captions found on the web. It separates "portrait" from "landscape" reliably. It is close to blind to the distinctions a specialist makes — period, hand, workshop, state of an engraving — because those words almost never appeared next to a picture in what it read.
  • "Similar" means similar to a particular model trained for a particular purpose. It agrees with human judgement often, but not always, and there is no signal that tells you which of the two you are looking at. When an answer surprises you, the model being wrong is as likely as a discovery.
  • The biases in the training material pass through intact. What the model finds noteworthy, typical or related reflects what was common on the web, not what is true of your collection or of the world.
  • Nothing here is a measurement. These are readings from one instrument with a known character. They are most valuable when they make you look at an image you would not otherwise have looked at — and least valuable when quoted as a number.
