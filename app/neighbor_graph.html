How the neighbor graph works

What it answers

The scatter plot answers where does this image sit in the collection overall. It cannot reliably answer which images is this one actually closest to, because turning a 512- or 1024-dimensional embedding into two dimensions has to throw information away. Two works can land next to each other on the map without being similar at all, simply because the projection had nowhere else to put them.

This view computes the relationships before that flattening. Each image is compared with every other image in the model's own space, and linked to its closest matches. A line here means the model really does consider those two images alike.

How it is built

  1. Load the vectors. The graph reuses the embedding file the selected projection was made from. Nothing is re-encoded and no model is loaded, which is why it takes about a second.
  2. Compare everything with everything. Similarity is the cosine between two vectors: 1.0 means identical direction, 0 means unrelated. This is the same measure text search uses.
  3. Keep the top k per image. Each image proposes a link to its k closest matches.
  4. Merge the pairs. If A picked B and B picked A, that is one link, marked mutual and drawn slightly heavier. A link only one side proposed is drawn lighter.
  5. Apply your filters — the similarity threshold and the reciprocal-only switch — and then work out which images ended up connected to which.

Communities: how the collection divides

Connected components only separate images with no path between them at all. Once k is high enough almost everything ends up in one component, and colouring by it tells you nothing — one colour for the whole screen.

Communities answer the question components cannot. The algorithm (Louvain) looks for groups whose members link to each other far more than they link outward, and keeps refining the division until no move improves it. It works inside a fully connected graph, so it divides your collection even when every image is reachable from every other.

What you get from it. This is the graph's own opinion about how the collection is organised, arrived at without the 2D projection and without K-Means. Comparing it with the cluster colours is the useful part: where the two agree, the grouping is solid; where they disagree, the projection or the clustering has distorted something. The modularity figure says how clear-cut the division is — roughly, below 0.3 the collection is fairly homogeneous and the groups are weak, above 0.5 they are strongly distinct. Communities also change with k and the similarity threshold, which is itself informative: a division that survives a range of settings is a real one.

Bridges and the images that hold the graph together

A bridge is a link whose removal would split the graph in two. It is the single connection holding two groups together, and in a collection it usually marks the work that belongs to both worlds — the landscape with a figure in it, the drawing that shares a technique with one group and a subject with another. Bridges are drawn heavier and in a distinct colour, and listed in the Structure panel.

An articulation point is the same idea applied to an image rather than a link: remove that one work and the graph falls apart. Those images are marked with a ring. Both are computed exactly, not estimated, and both change as you move k and the threshold.

Projection stress: where the 2D map lies

In the Projection coordinates layout every link has two measurements that ought to agree: how far apart the two images sit on screen, and how similar the model says they are. When you colour edges by projection stress, the warm ones are the links where they disagree most — strongly similar images that the projection pushed far apart.

Those are exactly the places where the scatter plot is misleading you, and they are unavoidable: any projection from hundreds of dimensions into two has to break some relationships. This just shows you which ones, so you know where not to trust the map.

Reading the picture

  • A tight knot is a group of works the model finds interchangeable: a series, a set of variants, or duplicates.
  • A hub — a node many links converge on — is a typical, representative example of its group. Colour by Degree to find hubs quickly.
  • A bridge — a single link joining two otherwise separate knots — is often the most interesting thing on screen: the work that connects two visual worlds.
  • An isolated node has no link left after filtering. It is an outlier: nothing else in the collection is close to it.
  • Separate islands (components) mean the collection genuinely splits into groups that share nothing at this value of k.

How the settings change what you see

Neighbours per image (k)
The single most important control. At k=1 you see only each image's single best match and the graph breaks into many pairs. At k=10 nearly everything joins one mass. Raise it until the structure you care about appears, then stop.
Minimum similarity
Cuts links below a confidence level. Use it when the graph is a hairball: raise the threshold until only relationships you would defend survive. Images whose links are all cut become isolated.
Only reciprocal edges
k forces every image to pick someone, even an outlier with no real neighbour. Requiring both sides to have chosen each other removes those one-sided guesses.
Layout
Projection coordinates keeps every node where the 2D map put it, so you can see directly where the map misleads: long links mean the projection separated images the model considers close. Force-directed ignores the map and lets the links arrange themselves, which is better for seeing the group structure on its own terms.
Color nodes by
Component shows the graph's own grouping. Cluster shows the K-Means labels from the projection — where the two disagree, one of them is wrong about the collection. Degree shades hubs warm and fringe nodes cool.

Interacting

Click a node to dim everything except its neighbourhood; the panel on the right lists its neighbours ranked by similarity, and clicking one of those jumps to it. Scroll zooms towards the cursor, drag pans. F fits the graph, R resets the view, B rebuilds it, and Esc clears the selection.

Limits worth knowing

  • Capped at 5000 images. Beyond that, build the graph from a subfolder, or use the grid mosaic.
  • Similarity is only as meaningful as the model that produced the embeddings. A Visual Features projection groups by colour and texture; a CLIP projection groups by subject matter.
  • An image whose embedding vector is unusable is kept in place but treated as unrelated to everything, and reported in the status message.
